Reading + reflection 5: The Rising Risk, and How We Stop It

Hennigan's The Rising Risk, and How We Stop It isn't really your typical news article. Because of the mix of animations, photos, and graphics, the whole story feels way more immersive. Itʼs crazy how much those digital tools can change a non-fiction piece, making a massive global issue feel real and personal instead of just something you're reading on a screen. The use of animation that relies on the readers pace in scrolling and reading is very effective. As readers scroll through the page, they see a missile launch, flight, and explosion happening in real time. Instead of just reading plainly about a detonation, the audience experiences it step by step or panel by panel. The slow movement of the missile builds this type of suspense, focusing more on the contrast of how quickly and yet slow an attack or an explosion could happen. With the addition of the animations of the fireball and shock wave visually push the text deeper into the devestation and brings awearness of these horrible thing much more than text could on its own. The article also has powerful illustrations and photographs. I say this because of how whatever was said in the article the artist takes those words and create images that further let the words have a bigger impact. Images of neighborhoods being consumed by fire, radioactive fallout in dark skies, and even desolate landscapes that show readers the very real tragedy of war and violence. At a point when i saw these pictures match the text so closely that they turn abstract descriptions into something much more real it moved me so much. With the photographs of actual government buildings or facilities and historical events add weight to the reporting, reminding the audience that nuclear policy is very very real and it affects real places and including people that make decisions every day. The charts and maps are actually a huge help for making sense of all the heavy data. Like, being able to see the trends in nuclear stockpiles from the Cold War all the way to today is so much easier than just reading about it. It also makes it really clear how the number of nuclear countries has actually grown, even though there are fewer weapons overall. But the most eye opening part for me were the interactive maps, seeing how food and economic shortages play out shows that a single strike would cause chaos way beyond the blast zone. The maps and graphs also make it a lot easier to actually process all that complex data. For instance, looking at the charts tracking nuclear stockpiles from the start of the Cold War to now helps you see historical trends much better than just reading about them. Itʼs also super weird to see that the number of countries with nuclear weapons has gone up, even if the total amount of weapons themselves has gone down. But the part that really got to me was the interactive maps, seeing how food and money shortages would play out shows that a strike would cause chaos way beyond the initial blast zone. I think the maps and charts are actually what make all that heavy data manageable. Seeing the trends in nuclear stockpiles from the Cold War through to today was way easier than just reading text. It was also pretty interesting to see how nuclear countries have increased, even if the total number of weapons actually went down. But for me, the interactive maps were the real standout, they show how things like food and economic shortages would cause chaos way beyond just the initial strike
